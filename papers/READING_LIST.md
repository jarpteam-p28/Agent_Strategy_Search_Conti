## 2. Recommended research — ES / NQ / index futures

### P1 · Pinning in the S&P 500 Futures (2012)
*Benjamin Golez, Jens Carsten Jackwerth* · instruments: ES, S&P 500 futures, S&P 500 futures options · access: unknown · testable on our data: partial

- **Why:** Finding: on expiry days the futures drift toward the nearby strike. Structural WHY: market makers have to re-hedge deltas as time decay pulls them in, and retail resells or early-exercises ITM options. That is a forced, predictable flow (the 'execution mechanics of big players' pattern). It is the only paper on this list that ties a specific expiry-day calendar to a price level, so it can be turned into simple rules. Possible rule: fade the move toward or away from the nearest 5- or 25-point strike in the last 1-2 hours on monthly/weekly expiry days. The edge would weaken if dealer hedging shifts to other instruments or if 0DTE volume swamps the pinning flow. Decay risk is real because it was published 2012 and 0DTE has changed the picture; test pre/post 2022.
- **Should contain:** Tests of whether S&P 500 futures are pulled toward the at-the-money strike on serial-option expiry days (pinning). Also tests the push away from the cost-of-carry-adjusted strike just before index-option expiry (anti-cross-pinning). Expect expiry-day price paths relative to the nearest strike, and an estimate of the hedge flow in notional dollars (at least $115M per expiration day). Data needed: option strikes and open interest by expiry. We have ES 1-min bars (2013-2026) and could build a strike grid ourselves, but we do not have option open interest.
- **Get it:** Open the SSRN abstract page (abstract_id=1664261) and click 'Download This Paper' (SSRN's free PDF button). If it is not offered, use the published JFE version, which is paywalled. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1664261
- **Save as:** `papers/inbox/pinning_in_the_s_p_500_futures.pdf`

### P1 · Stock Returns over the FOMC Cycle (2019)
*Anna Cieslak, Adair Morse, Annette Vissing-Jorgensen* · instruments: S&P 500 index, ES · access: open_pdf · testable on our data: yes

- **Why:** Finding: since 1994 the entire equity premium was earned in even weeks counted from the last FOMC meeting. WHY (the authors' causal story): news about unexpectedly accommodating monetary policy reached the market, partly via informal Fed communication, on a predictable calendar (information-flow plus Fed-put pattern). That gives a simple calendar rule: be long ES in weeks 0/2/4/6 of the FOMC cycle and flat or neutral in odd weeks, with fixed holding periods and a time exit at the end of each week. It fits the desk's earlier note that scheduled-event drifts should be conditioned on volatility. What would kill the edge: the Fed stops leaking or tightens communication, or the Fed put gets priced in. Decay risk is high because the paper was public from 2015 (SSRN) and the Fed review notes it was unknown before. Treat the ES post-2016 sample as the real out-of-sample test.
- **Should contain:** Average excess returns by week of the FOMC cycle (weeks 0, 2, 4, 6 versus odd weeks), 1994-2016. The CFA Digest summary reports even-week excess returns of about 0.33-0.60% and odd-week returns of about -0.09% to -0.18%. Also event studies on intermeeting rate changes, Fed funds futures, and the channel of informal Fed communication with the media and financial sector. We only need a list of FOMC meeting dates to test it on ES daily data (2013-2026). Check the 2017-2026 out-of-sample stretch separately.
- **Get it:** Download directly from the Berkeley Haas faculty PDF link. The SSRN abstract page (abstract_id=2687614) also has a 'Download This Paper' button. The published JFE version is Wiley DOI 10.1111/jofi.12818 (paywalled). https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2687614
- **Save as:** `papers/inbox/stock_returns_over_the_fomc_cycle.pdf`

### P1 · The Price Impact of Hedging Expiring Index Options (2026)

### P1 · Turn-of-the-Month: Window Dressing Behavior (2015)
*Linus Nilsson* · instruments: S&P 500 futures, Russell 2000 futures, equity indices · access: ssrn_free_download · testable on our data: yes

- **Why:** Finding: there is a meaningfully negative expected return from owning equities in the last trading hour of the month. The effect is bigger in US small-cap indices than large-cap. It had persisted for over a decade at the time. WHY pattern is institutional constraint or mandate. Fund managers are tied to monthly reporting cycles, and the author also lists risk control and lottery-ticket behavior as possible causes. This is a calendar-specific intraday effect that is simple to code. The paper dates from 2015 and our ES data runs from Dec 2013 to Sep 2026, so almost the whole sample is a clean post-publication out-of-sample test. This is a good test of decay. What would kill the edge: if reporting incentives fade, or if rebalancing flows turn the effect positive in strong-equity months. Test it conditional on the month's return, as in Harvey et al. - **Should contain:** Average returns in the last trading hour of month-end days against all other days, across several indices. The author also proposes comparing last-day returns with average days using S&P 500 and Russell 2000 futures. Expect t-stats, sub-period splits and a small-cap vs large-cap comparison. Our ES 1-min ETH/RTH data covers the S&P 500 futures leg directly. The Russell leg is not available (no RTY).

### P2 · 0DTEs: Trading, Gamma Risk and Volatility Propagation (2025)
*Chukwuma Dim, Bjorn Eraker, Grigory Vilkov* · instruments: S&P 500 index, ES · access: ssrn_free_download · testable on our data: partial

- **Why:** Finding: market makers' net gamma in S&P 500 0DTE options is on average positive and negatively related to future intraday volatility. Positive gamma strengthens intraday reversal, and negative gamma strengthens momentum. The authors say the evidence fits delta-hedging and not information-based trading. WHY pattern is execution mechanics of big players (dealer delta-hedging is forced). It is the regime-switch paper behind our gamma-level work. Use it to frame a 'momentum vs reversal depends on dealer gamma' test. It does not duplicate the known Adams et al. paper; it is the earlier, separate version of the same research line, with its own tests. What would kill the edge: a change in who holds the 0DTE book, for example retail turning into net sellers, which flips the sign of dealer gamma. Decay risk is moderate because the idea is widely discussed among practitioners.
- **Should contain:** Regressions of intraday realized volatility, and of momentum or reversal returns, on market-maker net gamma estimated from SPX/SPXW option data. ### P2 · Intraday time series momentum: Global evidence and links to market characteristics (2022)
*Zeming Li, Athanasios Sakkas, Andrew Urquhart* · instruments: index futures and ETFs, 16 developed markets, S&P 500 · access: open_pdf · testable on our data: yes

- **Why:** Finding: the first half-hour return predicts the last half-hour return in 12 of 16 developed markets. The effect is stronger when liquidity is low, volatility is high and information arrives discretely. The authors also find that the US first half-hour return predicts the local last half-hour return in other countries. WHY pattern is a mix of slow information diffusion, liquidity provision and infrequent rebalancing. The conditioning variables (volatility, liquidity, signal strength) are what you can build into simple rules. The paper has out-of-sample analysis. It extends the known Gao et al. paper and the Baltussen et al. paper beyond the US, so it is a cross-market robustness check rather than a duplicate. What would kill the edge: structural shifts such as 0DTE dealer gamma, which Rosa (2022) already links to the disappearance of the effect after publication. Test pre/post 2022 and by volatility regime.
- **Should contain:** Predictive regressions of last half-hour returns on first half-hour (and second-to-last half-hour) returns for each market, with in-sample and out-of-sample R2. Also a global ITSM portfolio with alphas, and tables linking ITSM strength to liquidity, volatility and information discreteness.
