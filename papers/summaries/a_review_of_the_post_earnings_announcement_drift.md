# A Review of the Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift

**Authors:** Josef Fink  
**Year:** 2020  **Type:** review  **Read:** full_pdf  
**Source:** https://static.uni-graz.at/fileadmin/sowi/Working_Paper/2020-04_Fink.pdf

> Stocks keep drifting for weeks to months in the direction of an earnings surprise (about 2% per side over 60 days historically, roughly 4% a quarter long-short), because information uncertainty, investor inattention, naive expectations and limits to arbitrage slow the price adjustment. The effect is strongest in small, illiquid, hard-to-arbitrage stocks and has weakened over time.

## Findings
- Stocks drift in the direction of the earnings surprise (SUE) for about 60 trading days after the announcement. The original evidence is a zero-investment long top-decile / short bottom-decile SUE portfolio. The drift is strongest in the first quarter after the announcement and persists for 2-3 quarters. — *Bernard & Thomas (1989): roughly +2%/-2% over 60 days, about 4% per quarter or 18% annualized abnormal return, long-short. Much of the drift is concentrated in the three-day window around the next quarter's announcement.*
- PEAD is found in most countries, developed and emerging. A few markets (Belgium, Singapore) show none, and markets with very weak institutions show none. — *Country studies listed in Observation 1; Griffin, Kelly & Nardari (2010) find the magnitude is surprisingly stable across markets.*
- The magnitude has been declining in the US. — *Chordia et al. 2014, Martineau 2019, Richardson et al. 2010. Suggested causes are lower limits to arbitrage, more arbitrage activity, internet-era information and more sophisticated processing of earnings news.*
- The drift is inversely related to firm size and is concentrated in stocks with high transaction costs, low liquidity, low volume and high idiosyncratic volatility. Size is largely a catch-all for these frictions. — *Observations 4 and 8. Studies disagree on whether profits survive costs: Chordia 2009, Ng 2008 and Zhang 2013 find about zero after costs; Battalio & Mendenhall 2011 and Ke & Ramalingegowda 2005 find significant profits.*
- Surprise measures can be built three ways: time-series (seasonal random walk) forecasts, analyst-consensus forecasts, and the announcement-window abnormal return. Analyst-based drift is larger and comes more from the long side. Combining time-series and analyst surprises (two-way sort) gives a stronger drift. Revenue surprises and cash-flow-driven surprises add incremental power. — *Livnat & Mendenhall 2006; Doyle et al. 2006; Jegadeesh & Livnat 2006; Shivakumar 2006; Chan et al. 1996.*
- PEAD is a separate anomaly. It is not explained by risk (CAPM, Fama-French and related factors), and it subsumes price momentum and other short-term anomalies. — *Observations 6 and 7; Daniel, Hirshleifer & Sun 2020 find that none of 12 short-horizon anomalies survive controlling for PEAD. Liquidity risk and idiosyncratic-volatility (arbitrage-risk) channels explain part of it.*
- The drift is stronger when attention is low or information is uncertain. Examples: announcements on Fridays, many concurrent announcements, high-volatility or down-market days, and conglomerates or internationally diversified firms. It is weaker with high institutional (especially active) ownership, conference calls, XBRL, options trading and analyst responsiveness. Passive ownership increases it. — *Observations 9, 11 and 19: DellaVigna & Pollet 2009; Hirshleifer et al. 2009; Ke & Ramalingegowda 2005; Qin & Singal 2015; Kimbrough 2005.*
- Investors, and to a lesser extent analysts, underestimate the autocorrelation of seasonally differenced quarterly earnings. This autocorrelation is positive at lags 1-3 and negative at lag 4. As a result, returns around the next announcements are predictable, with a reversal at q+4. — *Bernard & Thomas 1990; Ball & Bartov 1996 (about 50% underestimation); Rendleman et al. 1987. Naive expectations are mostly found among small investors.*
- Behavioral amplifiers: the disposition effect, anchoring on the 52-week high (stronger drift for good news near the high and bad news far from it), overconfidence, and inflation illusion. Unsophisticated arbitrageurs can also overcorrect, which reverses the drift in heavily arbitraged names (stocks with active options). — *Frazzini 2006; George et al. 2015; Chordia & Shivakumar 2005; Milian 2015.*

## The WHY
**Pattern:** Slow information diffusion plus liquidity/arbitrage limits (playbook patterns: slow information diffusion, liquidity constraints on large orders), with an added behavioral underreaction layer (inattention, naive earnings-autocorrelation expectations)

An earnings release carries information about future earnings. Many investors treat earnings as a seasonal random walk and so underestimate their persistence. Attention is limited on busy days, Fridays and for complex firms. When signals are noisy, rational learning also delays updating. Informed or institutional buyers cannot build a full position at once in illiquid names, because of impact costs, position limits and idiosyncratic risk, so they spread buying over weeks. The price therefore adjusts in steps, with extra jumps at later announcements as the market is surprised again in the same direction.

- **Who is forced / predictable:** Unsophisticated and retail investors who anchor on seasonal-random-walk expectations and are inattentive. Analysts who underreact and herd. Institutions with position, industry and turnover limits and with concentrated ownership, which are reluctant to add to names they are already overweight. Arbitrageurs who face transaction costs, short-sale limits and idiosyncratic-volatility risk, which keeps them from fully closing the gap in small, illiquid names.
- **Why it persists:** Limits to arbitrage are structural: costs, idiosyncratic risk and capacity are concentrated in small, illiquid, low-coverage stocks. Behavioral biases and attention limits are persistent. The surprise is genuinely multidimensional (revenue, cash flow, guidance, call tone, linked firms), so new variants keep appearing. A combination of factors, not one, drives it. The anomaly has lasted 50+ years despite wide publication.
- **What would kill it:** Faster and more complete processing of earnings news by algorithms and NLP, and by active institutions and HFT liquidity providers. Cheaper trading and shorting in small caps. Regulation or disclosure changes that cut information uncertainty (XBRL, more guidance). Heavy crowding by arbitrageurs, which pushes the drift toward overreaction (Milian 2015). Data providers cutting their dissemination delay. The paper itself cites evidence that the effect is attenuating, but not that it has disappeared.
