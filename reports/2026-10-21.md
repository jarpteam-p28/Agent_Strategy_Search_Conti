It has no trading rules, so it gives no tradable edge we don't already have. Breaking it down:
(1) The main finding, a 12-month run-up before the earnings report, is a hindsight artefact. Firms are sorted by a surprise that is only known after the report, and the authors read the run-up as efficient pricing, not as an opportunity. Nothing tells you when to trade before the report.
(2) The only tradable part is the small drift after the report, about 1-2 months. The paper itself measures it at roughly +0.3% for good news and -1.0% for bad news, which is inside the ~1% transaction costs the authors cite. Our existing pead_long strategy already covers this drift: long only, small and mid caps, a 60-day hold, and a positive surprise plus a positive price reaction. pead_next_announcement covers the related repeat-surprise effect.
(3) This paper's one new ingredient is a cruder surprise measure: earnings change versus last year (the 'naive' model). That is a variant or baseline test inside pead_long ("does a consensus-based surprise add anything over the naive year-over-year surprise?"), not a separate strategy.
(4) The index-futures angle (an earnings-season regime filter for ES/NQ) has no support in the paper and no clear WHY at index level. Under our playbook it is a curiosity, not an edge.
(5) Our data can't test it anyway: no single stocks, earnings dates, EPS or consensus estimates.
Recommendation: add the naive year-over-year surprise as a baseline variant to pead_long, and cite this paper as its 1968 source (the price-reaction leg).) |
